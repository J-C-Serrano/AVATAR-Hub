# AVATAR Hub — Voice recording script (fine-tuning)

Purpose: about **25 minutes of clean speech** that shows everything that makes your voice yours: your accent,
your rhythm, how you stress words, how you explain things, your fillers and habits. Reading the whole
session at a relaxed pace takes **35–40 minutes**, including breaks.

You do **not** need to read perfectly. If you stumble, pause and say the sentence again. Every recording
is transcribed automatically, so the text does not have to match the script exactly.
**Do not try to sound "neutral" or "correct". Speak exactly as you do when you lecture.**

---

## Before you start (5 minutes, important)

| Do | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Quiet room with soft surfaces (curtains, sofa, carpet) | Empty rooms that echo, open windows, fans, AC, music |
| The best microphone you have: a USB mic or a headset mic beats the webcam | Laptop or webcam microphone if anything better is available |
| Mic 15–25 cm from your mouth, slightly to the side (avoids "p" pops) | Moving closer and further away during the session |
| Same place, same mic, same distance for the whole session | Recording parts on different days with different setups |
| WAV, 48 kHz (or 44.1 kHz), mono is fine | MP3, voice-message apps, noise suppression or "enhance" filters |
| Water nearby; take a 1-minute break between sections | Reading 40 minutes straight (the voice gets tired and flat) |
| Leave 1 second of silence at the start and end of each file | Talking over clicks or keyboard noise |

**Level check:** say a loud sentence ("This is really important!"). The meter should peak around −6 dB and never
hit 0 dB (red). If it clips, lower the input gain.

**Files:** one file per section is ideal (`A_accent.wav`, `B_sounds.wav`, …). Put them in
`samples/Julio Serrano/voice_session_YYYY-MM-DD/`.

## Section A — Accent paragraphs (≈2 min)

Read each paragraph once at normal speed, then once more a little faster, as if you were in a hurry.

**A1.** (Speech Accent Archive elicitation paragraph, © Steven Weinberger, George Mason University,
accent.gmu.edu, used for research under its Creative Commons terms.)

> Please call Stella. Ask her to bring these things with her from the store: six spoons of fresh snow peas,
> five thick slabs of blue cheese, and maybe a snack for her brother Bob. We also need a small plastic snake
> and a big toy frog for the kids. She can scoop these things into three red bags, and we will go meet her
> Wednesday at the train station.

**A2.**

> Julio Serrano works in Finland, but his story starts far from the Baltic Sea. He thinks that the
> things that matter most are rarely the things that are easiest to measure. This Thursday, three of his
> students will show their thesis work: one about value, one about very large vehicle fleets, and one about
> the way services change what a factory is worth. "Think big, but check the details," he usually says.

**A3.**

> Yesterday I visited a machine shop where every sheet of steel is cut by a laser. The shift
> supervisor showed me a chart: fifty-seven machines, nineteen hours of daily use, and almost zero unplanned
> stops. "Six years ago," she said, "this was a very different place. We just sold equipment. Now we sell
> availability, and that changes everything: the contract, the data, the people, even the jokes in the
> coffee room."

## Section C — Lecture passages (≈8 min)

Read as if you were teaching a class: pause where you'd pause, stress what you'd stress.

**C1. Servitization**

> Let's start with a simple question: what does a manufacturing company actually sell? For most of the
> twentieth century, the answer was obvious. It sold products: machines, engines, trucks, elevators. The
> customer paid once, took the product home, and the relationship more or less ended there. Servitization
> changes that logic. Instead of selling a machine, the company sells what the machine does: uptime,
> performance, availability, or even a guaranteed outcome. Think about jet engines sold as "power by the
> hour", or compressors sold as cubic meters of compressed air. The customer pays for results, and the
> provider takes on part of the risk.

**C2. Why it is hard**

> Now, this sounds attractive, but it is not easy. First, the business model changes: revenue becomes
> recurring, but costs move earlier and the provider has to finance the equipment. Second, the
> organisation changes: sales people used to closing a deal now have to manage a relationship for ten
> years. Third, data becomes critical. You cannot promise ninety-eight percent availability if you don't
> know, in real time, how your machines are behaving. And fourth, trust: the customer has to believe that
> you will be there when something goes wrong, at three in the morning, on a Sunday.

**C3. Numbers, dates and names** (read the numbers in full, the way you naturally say them)

> In 2019, the company had 1,250 employees and revenue of 340 million euros. By 2024, services represented
> 42 percent of revenue, up from 18 percent. The average contract lasted 7.5 years. Response time dropped
> from 48 hours to 6 hours. The project started on the 3rd of March and ended on the 21st of November.
> Partners included Wärtsilä, Kone, Volvo, ABB, Siemens and the University of Vaasa. The research team
> interviewed 64 managers in 11 countries, from Finland and Sweden to Spain, Mexico and Brazil.

**C4. A case, told like a story**

> Imagine a small Finnish company that makes industrial pumps. For decades, they did one thing very well:
> build reliable pumps and sell them through distributors. Then a large customer called and said: "We don't
> want pumps anymore. We want water to move from here to there, every day, with no surprises." At first,
> the managing director laughed. Then he stopped laughing, because he realised the customer was serious, and
> that a competitor would say yes if he said no. So they said yes. They installed sensors, they trained
> technicians, they rewrote the contracts. The first year was painful. The third year was profitable. By the
> fifth year, the pumps were almost a detail.

**C5. Wrapping up a lecture**

> So, what should you remember from today? Three things. One: servitization is not about adding a few
> services to a product; it is about changing what you promise to the customer. Two: the hardest part is
> not technology, it is the organisation and the culture. And three: done well, it creates value for both
> sides, the provider and the customer, over a very long time. For next week, please read the two articles
> on the course page, and come ready to discuss them. Any questions? No? Perfect. See you on Thursday.

## Section D — Expression and attitude (≈3 min)

Read each one with the feeling in brackets. **Exaggerate a little**: the model learns your range from this.

1. (curious) Have you ever wondered why some companies survive every crisis?
2. (surprised) Wait, really? Forty percent in only two years?
3. (serious) This is the part that most companies get wrong.
4. (enthusiastic) And this is where it gets really, really interesting!
5. (calm, slow) Let's take a moment and look at this step by step.
6. (doubtful) Hmm, I'm not completely sure that's the right conclusion.
7. (friendly) Good morning everyone, and welcome back. I hope you had a nice weekend.
8. (firm) No. That's not acceptable, and I'll explain why.
9. (joking) Of course, in theory everything works. In practice... well, it's Monday.
10. (warm) Thank you all for your work this semester. I really enjoyed it.
11. (listing) We have three options: wait, partner, or build it ourselves.
12. (question, rising) Is it a product? Is it a service? Or is it both?
13. (emphasis on the bold word) I said **outcomes**, not outputs.
14. (emphasis on the bold word) It's not the **machine** that matters, it's the **result**.
15. (whisper-quiet, then normal) Here's a secret... most of this is common sense.

## Section E — Free speech (≈6 min, the most important part for your habits)

No script. Answer each question for **about one minute**, as if talking to a student or a colleague.
Use your normal fillers ("so…", "well…", "you know…", "eh…"), restarts and jokes. **This part teaches the model how
*you* talk, not how you read.**

1. Introduce yourself: who you are, where you are from, how you ended up in Finland.
2. Explain servitization to a first-year student who has never heard the word.
3. Tell a story about a company or a project that surprised you.
4. What do you like and dislike about teaching?
5. Describe your perfect weekend.
6. Give your opinion on AI avatars in education: good idea, bad idea, or both?

---

## Optional Section F — other lecture languages (≈2 min each)

If you lecture in **Finnish** or **Swedish**, read or improvise 2 minutes in each, ideally explaining the same
idea as C1. This keeps your accent consistent across languages in the multilingual model.
Skip this if you only want English for now.
